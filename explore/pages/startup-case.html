Internal memo for the team / DubHacks NEXT, Commit Fellowship

The company is the perception, verification and skill-scoring layer. The arm is the demo.

Thesis. We sell software that identifies instruments, verifies counts and trays, and scores surgical skill, running on cheap cameras and arms. We start where FDA does not apply (training, then sterile processing), earn a cleared counting aid next, and treat a purpose-built handoff robot as the long-term vision, not the first product. The $250 hobby arm cannot be the clinical device, and we say so first.

Answer to "medical isn't consumer ready": the first revenue comes from buyers with no premarket review. Everything with a patient in the loop is staged behind evidence and FDA feedback.

02

Is medical a dealbreaker?

No, if we sequence it. Regulation is set by intended use, not by the hardware. A scoring tool used on props is outside FDA review. A counting aid has three cleared precedents. Only the robot that hands instruments in a live OR has no predicate and needs De Novo, which is a multi-year, multi-million program.

06

Three company shapes

Defensible plan: Shape 2 to first revenue, then Shape 1 or 3 as the funded follow-on. Shape 2 is fastest and safest. Shape 1 has the clearest ROI. Shape 3 has the biggest end state and the longest road.

Shape 2 / start here

Surgical training hardware plus SaaS

Lowest regulatory burden, eager institutional buyer, builds the labeled skill dataset. The arm demonstrates, the camera scores.

First product
SutureCoach on existing box trainers (direction 13), arm demo as premium tier. $500 to $2,000 kit plus per-seat subscription
First customer
One surgical-technology program and one general surgery residency sim lab
18-month milestone
10 programs, 300 active learners, validation study vs blinded OSATS submitted, $200K to $400K ARR
Risk: small market (about $50M/yr US-plus), long-term value is data, not revenue.
Shape 1 / likely revenue engine

SPD automation company

Most automatable corner of the hospital: fixed bins, known catalogs, about 40% of tech time on tray assembly, no patient contact.

First product
Set-reconciliation camera station (direction 8), then a low-cost pick-and-assemble cell for the 20 most common sets
First customer
A mid-size community hospital or reprocessing contractor with a 15 to 20 person SPD
18-month milestone
3 paying sites, over 99% verified tray accuracy on 10 set types over 5,000 trays, minutes saved per tray documented, $300K to $500K ARR
Risk: Censis and RIF Robotics exist; real instruments are shiny look-alikes; slow hospital procurement.
Shape 3 / biggest end state

OR perception software, arm later

Ceiling and tray cameras sold as an OR quality and efficiency product, with a cleared counting adjunct next, then the arm as actuator.

First product
OR Case Board plus preference-card optimizer (directions 20 and 21), count verification second
First customer
One ambulatory surgery center chain (fast procurement, throughput-driven)
18-month milestone
5 ORs live, 2,000 cases recorded with privacy controls, measured tray-size reduction, FDA pre-sub for the count module, first arm pilot in a sim lab
Risk: longest road, hospital IT and privacy gates, bundling by Stryker and Medline.
07

Staged roadmap

Each stage ends with a gate an investor can check. The robot appears last, only with a purpose-built medical-grade arm, a sterile concept and Series A capital.

Hackathon

Now to demo day
  • Demo on props; "training/research prototype, not for clinical use" on every screen
  • Capture per-instrument detection accuracy and handoff success over N trials, with the 5 mm caveat
  • 60-second video
  • Pitch the perception and training platform with a stated regulatory ladder
Gate: numbers on our own props, honest limits stated

3 months

Discovery and entity
  • 20 discovery calls: 8 sim labs, 6 SPD managers, 3 device-training heads, 3 vet or dental
  • Form a Delaware C-corp; start SAM.gov registration (takes weeks)
  • Skill-scoring MVP on a box trainer under a data-use agreement at one lab
  • Apply: CoMotion gap fund (if UW-affiliated), NSF Project Pitch; book a regulatory consult
Gate: 2 to 3 pilot commitments, one paid; a named clinical advisor

12 months

Revenue and evidence
  • 3 to 5 paying training customers ($20K to $100K ARR, target)
  • 1 SPD pilot on real trays with measured error and time
  • IRB-approved or exempt study: automated scores vs OSATS/GEARS raters; submit an abstract
  • QMS-lite and IEC 62304 habits in the repo; FDA Q-Sub for the counting aid
  • Pre-seed $500K to $1.5M range plus NSF Phase I
Gate: revenue, clinical validation, FDA feedback in hand

36 months

Three branches
  • A, counting aid: 510(k) filed months 18 to 30; clearance near month 36 if no clinical trial
  • B, SPD: 20+ sites integrated with an instrument-management system; likely the revenue engine
  • C, robot: purpose-built arm, sterile drape concept, engineering partner or acquirer, Series A of $8M to $20M+ range; De Novo by year 5 to 6
  • ISO 13485 QMS, 3+ health-system LOIs, narrow provisional patents
Gate: cleared adjunct or 20 SPD sites

Five "what if" ideas that make a judge or investor lean in

The flight data recorder for the OR

Every case yields a privacy-safe, tamper-evident replay of every instrument and count. Insurers discount premiums; disputed counts are resolved by replay. The payer is the insurer.

Builds on 16, 19, 29
Surgery school in a shoebox

A $500 kit puts expert-scored practice into every residency and low-resource school; the arm demonstrates expert motion and a remote mentor can drive it from a phone.

Builds on 28, 20, 22. Fits the Community Kitchen track
The preference card that learns

The system observes what each surgeon actually uses and shrinks trays, with hard-dollar ROI on day one, no robot and no FDA. The wedge that pays for the moonshot. Claim the 20 to 30% only after measuring.

Builds on 32, 21, S7
A robot that never touches the patient

Safety is the product: the arm lives on the back table and in SPD only. "Augments, never replaces." Show palm gate, STOP and zones as the headline feature.

A master scrub tech or vet tech demonstrates a procedure ten times by teleop; it becomes a rated, purchasable skill that runs on any arm, with revenue share to the expert.

10

Risky claims to avoid on stage

Judges and investors probe safety, sterility and regulation first. The first five come from the surgeon research file; the last two come from the go-to-market file.

1
This robot eliminates retained surgical items.Causes are multifactorial; no device guarantees prevention.
Augments manual counting with redundant instrument tracking.
2
The FDA clearance path is straightforward.Robots may need novel-device routes (De Novo).
A research and training tool with a clear regulatory roadmap following the IDEAL framework.
3
It replaces surgical technologists and scrub nurses.Creates opposition from clinical staff.
Supports technologists by automating tedious tasks so they can focus on patient care and mentorship.
4
da Vinci-level precision at low cost.da Vinci is $1.5M+ and decades of development. Ours is about 5 mm repeatability, 0.3 kg payload.
Low-cost gesture recognition and basic manipulation for training applications.
5
This is ready for clinical use.Plastic props, no sterility, no certification.
A proof of concept for training and research, not clinical deployment.
6
The SO-101 is the product we will put in the OR.Hobby servos and printed links will not survive a safety case. Say it before an investor finds out.
The arm is the demo and a training accessory. The product is the perception and scoring layer.
7
Our detector works on real instruments.Plastic-prop accuracy does not transfer to shiny, near-identical forceps, wet gauze and blood.
Validated on props; we are collecting real-tray data with a partner SPD.

Also avoid: quoting a retained-item ROI as a promise (hospitals have heard it for 20 years and many bought RFID), the 95 to 100% video-scoring accuracy numbers (small within-dataset benchmarks), and any single TAM figure without a source.
